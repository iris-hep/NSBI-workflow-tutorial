\title{A hybrid neural simulation-based inference method for LHC analyses}

\author{Rafael Coelho Lopes de S\'a}
\affiliation{Department of Physics, University of Massachusetts, Amherst, Amherst 01003, USA}
\author{Jay Sandesara}
\affiliation{Data Science Institute, University of Wisconsin--Madison, Madison, Wisconsin 53706, USA}

\date{\today}

\begin{abstract}
Neural simulation-based inference based on classifier estimates of density ratios has made unbinned, high-dimensional likelihood-ratio inference practical for analyses with many physics processes and nuisance parameters.  The formulation based on density ratios, however, does not provide an explicit normalized event density or a direct generative model.  Consequently, expected-data calculations and frequentist pseudo-experiments must be represented by very large simulated samples, which can dominate the computational cost of profiling, uncertainty decomposition, and Neyman constructions. Normalizing flows provide normalized densities and efficient sampling, but small absolute-density errors can be consequential when a small signal is inferred in a much larger background. A \textbf{hybrid neural simulation-based inference} is proposed to address these shortcoming.  A normalizing flow is used as a tractable representation of a  physically representative reference distribution.  Events sampled from that learned reference form the denominator sample for classifier-based estimates of each process-to-reference density ratio.  A process density is then represented as the product of the tractable reference-flow density and its learned ratio.  This construction preserves the discriminative precision of density-ratio estimation while making every component of the event intensity tractable and sampleable.  The same fixed reference sample supplies normalized importance weights for process generation and a deterministic weighted, unbinned Asimov dataset.  The extended likelihood, the sampling prescription, and the Asimov construction are derived. This method provides a route from semi-parametrized density-ratio inference to a generative, likelihood-based analysis without requiring an independently precise absolute-density estimator for every process.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Likelihood-based measurements at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) conventionally compress reconstructed events into one or a few binned observables.  This construction is robust and computationally convenient, but the compression and binning can discard information when the likelihood depends nonlinearly on parameters or when no low-dimensional summary is sufficient over the full parameter space.  Neural simulation-based inference (NSBI) instead uses high-dimensional simulated events to estimate likelihoods or likelihood ratios directly in reconstructed feature space~\cite{Cranmer:2015bka,Brehmer:2018eca,Brehmer:2018hga}.

The ATLAS Collaboration developed a practical classifier-based NSBI formalism for LHC parameter estimation~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr} which was used in the measurement of off-shell Higgs-boson production in the $H^{*}\!\to ZZ\to4\ell$ final state~\cite{ATLAS:2024tgo}.  The formalism uses neural estimates of density ratios and an analytic factorization of the dependence on parameters of interest (POIs) and nuisance parameters.  It can therefore describe dozens of POIs and hundreds of nuisance parameters without densely sampling that complete parameter space during neural-network training.  This construction is known as semi-parametric NSBI.

Density ratios are sufficient for inference based on a profile likelihood ratio.  They are also often easier to estimate to the required precision than absolute densities.  A reference sample can be designed to resemble the relevant physics, sharing resonant structure, tails, and detector effects with the target samples.  The resulting ratios vary more mildly than either absolute density, and they can be learned discriminatively with a binary cross-entropy loss.  This numerical advantage was central to the precision achieved in the ATLAS studies.

A density-ratio-only representation nevertheless has limitations since it is not itself a generative density model.  An unbinned Asimov dataset must be approximated by a large weighted Monte Carlo (MC) sample, and pseudo-experiments must be constructed by resampling such a sample.  The former makes repeated profiling and impact calculations costly; the latter requires an MC pool much larger than a typical pseudo-experiment~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr}.  These difficulties do not invalidate ratio-based inference and they were successfully used in the ATLAS measurement of the off-shell Higgs production, but they make some standard LHC statistical workflows expensive.

Normalizing flows address the complementary problem.  They provide both a normalized, tractable density and efficient event generation~\cite{Rezende:2015flows,Papamakarios:2019flows}. The difficulty is precision: two separately trained process flows can each pass many marginal checks while their residual errors bias the density ratio between processes that controls a small-signal measurement.  Recent work has developed factorized parameter-dependent flows~\cite{Valsecchi:2026flows}, but the required absolute-density accuracy remains a central question when the signal is orders of magnitude smaller than the background.

The formalism presented in this paper combines the two representations.  A flow is used to build a normalized, sampleable reference density, while classifiers learn the precision-critical ratios of each process to that exact learned reference.  The reference flow is a proposal distribution, not an uncorrected model of every process.  If the ratio estimators are exact, local reference-flow error cancels algebraically as long as the flow provides support to the distributions.  The resulting \textit{hybrid NSBI} construction supplies a tractable likelihood, direct weighted or resampled event generation, and a deterministic unbinned Asimov measure.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.  \Cref{sec:parametric} reviews semi-parametrized NSBI and its practical limitations.  \Cref{sec:hybrid} introduces the hybrid construction.  \Cref{sec:sampling} develops weighted sampling and pseudo-experiments, and \Cref{sec:asimov} derives the weighted unbinned Asimov dataset.  \Cref{sec:demonstration} describes a five-dimensional proof-of-concept experiment.  Validation requirements are discussed in \Cref{sec:discussion} and conclusions in \Cref{sec:conclusion}.

\section{Semi-parametrized neural simulation-based inference}
\label{sec:parametric}

\subsection{Extended unbinned likelihood}

Let $ x\in\R^d$ denote the reconstructed observables for an event and let $s$ index physics samples.  For parameters $\theta=(\mu,\alpha)$, including POIs $\mu$ and nuisance parameters $\alpha$, define the differential event intensity
\begin{equation}
  \nu( x\mid\theta)
  =\sum_s \lambda_s(\theta)
    p_s( x\mid\theta),
  \label{eq:intensity}
\end{equation}
where $p_s$ is normalized to unity and $\lambda_s$ is the expected yield.  Its integral is the total expected yield:
\begin{equation}
  \Lambda(\theta)=\int \nu( x\mid\theta)\dd x
  =\sum_s\lambda_s(\theta).
\end{equation}
Up to factors independent of $\theta$, the extended unbinned likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \Lcal(\theta)
  =e^{-\Lambda(\theta)}
   \prod_{i=1}^{n}\nu( x_i\mid\theta)
   \prod_k \pi_k(a_k\mid\alpha_k),
  \label{eq:extended-likelihood}
\end{equation}
where $\pi_k$ are constraint terms for auxiliary measurements $a_k$.  The profile-likelihood-ratio statistic for a POI value $\mu$ is
\begin{equation}
  t_{\mu}
  =-2\log\frac{\Lcal(\mu,\widehat{\widehat{\alpha}}_{\mu})}
                       {\Lcal(\muhat,\widehat{\alpha})}.
  \label{eq:profile-statistic}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Classifiers as density-ratio estimators}
\label{sec:ratio}

Consider a classifier trained with equal total class weight to distinguish events from densities $p(x\mid y=1)$ and $p(x\mid 0)$.  At the population minimum of the binary cross-entropy loss, its score is
\begin{equation}
  s^*_{\psi}(y=1\mid x)=\frac{p(y=1)p(x\mid y=1)}{p(y=1)p(x\mid y=1)+p(y=0)p(x\mid y=0)}.
\end{equation}
For balanced samples $p(y=1)=p(y=0)$, the density ratio follows from the odds~\cite{Cranmer:2015bka},
\begin{equation}
  r^\psi( x)=\frac{p( x\mid y=1)}{p( x\mid y=0)}
  =\frac{s^*_{\psi}(y=1\mid x)}{1-s^*_{\psi}(y=1\mid x)}.
  \label{eq:classifier-ratio}
\end{equation}
Balanced class weights are important: otherwise the class-prior odds must be included explicitly. Calibration tests, reweighting closure tests, and maximum likelihood estimator closure tests are used to control finite-sample effects and optimization~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr}. 

In the density ratio-based NSBI, a parameter-independent reference density $\pRef( x)$ is chosen whose support covers all relevant components
\begin{equation}
  r_s( x\mid\theta)
  =\frac{p_s( x\mid\theta)}{\pRef( x)}.
  \label{eq:process-reference-ratio}
\end{equation}
A classifier $r_s^\psi$ is trained to approximate $r_s$ and the intensity may then be written as
\begin{equation}
  \nu( x\mid\theta)
  =\pRef( x)
   \sum_s\lambda_s(\theta)r_s^\psi( x\mid\theta).
  \label{eq:ratio-intensity}
\end{equation}
Because the reference is parameter independent, $\sum_i\log\pRef( x_i)$ cancels from every log likelihood ratio.  Hence the reference need not have a tractable density for the inference in \cref{eq:profile-statistic}; only the component-to-reference ratios are required.

\subsection{Limitations of a density ratio-only model}
\label{sec:ratio-limitations}

The cancellation of $\pRef( x)$ in \cref{eq:ratio-intensity} is sufficient for parameter estimation, but it also means that the construction does not specify a normalized density that can be evaluated or sampled.  Two consequences are particularly relevant.

First, there is no simple way to define an Asimov dataset meant to return the generating parameter without statistical fluctuation and to encode median expected sensitivity~\cite{Cowan:2010js}.  In a binned likelihood, the expected count is placed in every bin.  In an unbinned ratio-only analysis, one instead has to use a large weighted MC sample as numerical quadrature for the expected event measure.  The approximation improves as this sample grows, but evaluating hundreds of neural networks on millions of events and repeating the calculation for profiles or impact decompositions can be expensive.  The ATLAS implementation reports likelihood maximizations on the order of several hours and hundreds of gigabytes for the off-shell Higgs boson measurement~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr}.

Second, a Neyman construction requires samples from the model at each tested parameter point.  A ratio alone does not provide a direct sampler.  A valid workaround is to bootstrap from a much larger positive-weight reference pool, with probabilities proportional to event weights or learned ratios.  The ATLAS procedure assigns each reference event a Poisson-distributed integer multiplicity with mean equal to its Asimov weight~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr}.  This is statistically sound for the discrete MC approximation but requires a pool much larger than an individual pseudo-experiment, and the pool remains the resolution limit of the generator.

These are computational and representational limitations, not failures of density-ratio inference.  They motivate adding a generative reference model without surrendering the ratio precision that made semi-parametrized NSBI practical.

\section{Hybrid neural simulation-based inference}
\label{sec:hybrid}

\subsection{A learned reference as a proposal density}

Let $\qRef( x)$ be a normalized normalizing-flow density trained on a reference MC sample. After training, a large, dedicated sample is drawn from $\qRef$.  Crucially, this \textit{flow-generated} sample, not the original MC sample, is used as the denominator class for the density-ratio classifiers.  For every physics sample $s$, the classifier therefore estimates
\begin{equation}
  r_s^{\psi}( x)
  =\frac{p_s( x)}{\qRef( x)}.
  \label{eq:flow-reference-ratio}
\end{equation}
The hybrid process density is
\begin{equation}
  p_s( x)
  =\qRef( x)r_s^{\psi}( x).
  \label{eq:hybrid-density}
\end{equation}

Note that even if the flow approximates the intended reference sample imperfectly, $\qRef\neq p_{\text{ref}}$,  an exact classifier trained against samples from $\qRef$ still learns $p_s/\qRef$, and \cref{eq:hybrid-density} returns $p_s$ exactly.  Local flow error is corrected by the ratio.  The flow is not required to be an independently precise model of any $p_s$; it must be evaluable, sampleable, and nonzero wherever any target density is nonzero. All these properties can be easily achieved with a normalizing flow.

As highlighted in the ATLAS implementation~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr}, the support condition is essential.  If $p_s( x)>0$ where $\qRef( x)$ is negligibly small, the ratio becomes large, classifier learning is difficult, and importance sampling has low effective sample size.  A balanced, physics-informed mixture, with broad tails and resonance peaks mimicking the features in the physics samples, and explicit support diagnostics are therefore part of the statistical design.

\subsection{Tractable event intensity and likelihood}

With hybrid component densities, the event intensity becomes
\begin{equation}
  \nu( x\mid\theta)
  =\qRef( x)
   \sum_s\lambda_s(\theta)
   r_s^{\psi}( x\mid\theta).
  \label{eq:hybrid-intensity}
\end{equation}
Every term is now tractable.  Define
$h( x\mid \theta)=\sum_s\lambda_s(\theta)r_s^{\psi}( x\mid\theta)$.
For a fixed reference flow, the contribution $\sum_i\log\qRef( x_i)$ remains independent of $\theta$ and cancels from profile likelihood ratios.  Thus inference retains the original ratio-based numerical structure,
\begin{equation}
  -2\log\Lcal(\theta)
  ={}2\Lambda(\theta)
  -2\sum_i\log\qRef( x_i) \notag\\
  -2\sum_i\log h( x_i\mid\theta)
  -2\sum_k\log\pi_k(a_k\mid \alpha_k),
  \label{eq:hybrid-nll}
\end{equation}
while the explicit $\qRef$ factor is available whenever an absolute density is needed.

The same factorized POI and nuisance-parameter model in~\cite{ATLAS:2024rpr} can be used after replacing the implicit reference by $\qRef$.  Consequently, the hybrid proposal is orthogonal to whether the parameter dependence is described by analytic interpolation, a conditional neural network, or any other nonparametric estimator.  Developments aimed at relaxing the factorization assumptions can be incorporated without changing the reference-density layer.

\subsection{Finite-sample normalization}

For an exact normalized ratio,
\begin{equation}
  \E_{\bm x\sim\qRef}[r_s^{\psi}( x)]=1.
  \label{eq:ratio-normalization}
\end{equation}
Finite classifiers generally violate this equality slightly.  Given a fixed reference sample $\{ x_m\}_{m=1}^{M}\sim\qRef$, we impose
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde r_s^\psi( x_m)
  = \frac{r_s^{\psi}( x_m)}
  {M^{-1}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}r_s^{\psi}( x_\ell)}.
  \label{eq:empirical-ratio-normalization}
\end{equation}
This guarantees exact component normalization on the empirical reference quadrature.  It does not repair local ratio bias, so calibration and multidimensional reweighting tests remain necessary.  It does, however, decouple local-shape validation from a trivial normalization offset and is what makes the discrete Asimov stationarity exact in \Cref{sec:asimov}.

\section{Sampling and Neyman construction}
\label{sec:sampling}

The fixed flow reference sample can represent any component by importance weights $w_m^{(s)}=r_s^{\psi}( x_m)$. A well calibrated ratio will preserve the normalization of the sample, but a trivial overall normalization can always be corrected.

Weighted histograms or expectations calculated with these weights approximate integrals over $p_s(x)$.  To generate an unweighted component event, one may sample an index $m$ with probability $w_m^{(s)}$.  Alternatively, new proposal points can be drawn from the flow and resampled using freshly evaluated ratios.  Rejection sampling is also possible when a reliable finite upper bound on the ratio is available.

For a pseudo-experiment at parameter point $\theta$, one first draws the component counts independently,
\begin{equation}
  n_s\sim\Pois\!\left(\lambda_s(\theta)\right),
\end{equation}
and then draws $n_s$ indices according to $w_m^{(s)}$.  Concatenating the selected reference events gives an unweighted pseudo-dataset from the empirical hybrid model.  Repeating the likelihood fit yields the sampling distribution of $t_{\mu}$ needed for a Neyman construction~\cite{Neyman:1937}.

When unweighting is not possible, the quality of this generator can be summarized by the effective sample size
\begin{equation}
  M_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(s)}
  =\frac{(\sum_m r_s^\psi)^2}
        {\sum_m (r_s^{\psi})^2}.
  \label{eq:ess}
\end{equation}
Large ratio tails reduce $M_{\mathrm{eff}}$.  Monitoring the effective sample size provides a direct diagnostic of reference coverage.  However, unlike a fixed original MC pool, however, a flow proposal can generate additional independent reference points whenever the effective sample size must be enlarged.

\section{A weighted unbinned Asimov dataset}
\label{sec:asimov}

\subsection{Expected event measure}

An Asimov dataset is most naturally an expected event measure, not a particular random list of unweighted events.  At a generating point $\theta_A$, define
\begin{equation}
  \dd N_A( x)
  =\nu( x\mid\theta_A)\dd x.
  \label{eq:asimov-measure}
\end{equation}
For any integrable function $f$,
\begin{align}
  \int f( x)\dd N_A( x)
  & =\E_{\qRef}\!\left[
      f( x)\sum_s\lambda_s(\theta_A)
      r_s^\psi( x\mid\theta_A)
    \right] \notag\\
  & \simeq\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m^A f( x_m),
  \label{eq:asimov-quadrature}
\end{align}
with deterministic event weights
\begin{equation}
  w_m^A=\frac{1}{M}\sum_s
  \lambda_s(\theta_A)
  r_s^\psi( x_m\mid\theta_A).
  \label{eq:asimov-weights}
\end{equation}
The extended part is included automatically:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m^A
  =\sum_s\lambda_s(\theta_A)
  =\Lambda(\theta_A),
  \label{eq:asimov-total-yield}
\end{equation}
where the first equality is exact on the empirical quadrature when every component ratio is normalized.

The weighted Asimov log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \log\Lcal_A(\theta)
  =-\Lambda(\theta)
   +\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_m^A
    \log \nu( x_m\mid\theta) +\log\pi( a_A\mid\alpha),
  \label{eq:asimov-loglikelihood}
\end{equation}
with auxiliary data set to their expectations.  In the continuous limit, the expected score vanishes at $\theta_A$ under standard regularity conditions.  On the finite case, if the parameters modify only the component yields while the component shapes remain fixed (as is the case in semi-parametric NSBI), and each process-to-reference ratio is normalized on this same sample (either because the optimizer reached a very precise calibration or because the sample was explicitly calibrated), the finite weighted sum reproduces the total expected yield exactly. Consequently, the Asimov score vanishes exactly at the generating parameters even for finite $M$. For parameter-dependent shapes, this exact cancellation additionally requires the shape ratios to be consistently normalized on the same reference quadrature.

\section{Finite-sample Asimov construction and expected test-statistic scans}
\label{sec:asimov-quadrature}

The hybrid model makes it possible to construct an unbinned Asimov data set using a finite sample drawn from the tractable reference density.  Let the extended event intensity be written as
%
\begin{equation}
    \nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    q_{\phi}(\mathbf{x})\,
    H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}),
    \qquad
    H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    =
    \sum_j
    \lambda_j(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,
    r_j(\mathbf{x}),
    \label{eq:hybrid-intensity-asimov}
\end{equation}
%
where \(q_{\phi}\) is the normalized reference density, \(r_j=p_j/q_{\phi}\)
is the density ratio for process \(j\), and
\(\lambda_j(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is its expected yield.  The total
expected yield is
%
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    \int \nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\mathbf{x}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}
    \left[H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})\right].
\end{equation}

Consider an Asimov data set generated at
\(\boldsymbol{\theta}_A\).  The expectation of the extended unbinned
log likelihood under this model is
%
\begin{equation}
    \ell_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    -\Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    +
    \int
    \nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_A)
    \log \nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})\,d\mathbf{x},
    \label{eq:expected-log-likelihood}
\end{equation}
%
up to terms independent of \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\).  This expression
follows from the Poisson point-process identity
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}_{A}
    \left[
        \sum_{i=1}^{N} f(\mathbf{x}_i)
    \right]
    =
    \int
    \nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_A)
    f(\mathbf{x})\,d\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}
%
Auxiliary measurements and constraint terms can be included by adding
their expected log likelihood to Eq.~\eqref{eq:expected-log-likelihood}.

The expected likelihood-ratio test statistic is therefore
%
\begin{align}
    t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    &=
    -2\left[
        \ell_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
        -
        \ell_A(\boldsymbol{\theta}_A)
    \right]
    \nonumber\\
    &=
    2\left[
        \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})
        -
        \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta}_A)
    \right]
    +
    2\int
    \nu_A(\mathbf{x})
    \log
    \frac{\nu_A(\mathbf{x})}
         {\nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})}
    \,d\mathbf{x}.
    \label{eq:asimov-ts-intensity}
\end{align}
%
Using \(\nu_A=q_{\phi}H_A\) and
\(\nu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=q_{\phi}H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\), the
reference density cancels inside the logarithm, giving
%
\begin{equation}
    t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    2\left[
        \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})
        -
        \Lambda_A
    \right]
    +
    2\,
    \mathbb{E}_{q_{\phi}}
    \left[
        H_A(\mathbf{x})
        \log
        \frac{H_A(\mathbf{x})}
             {H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}
    \right].
    \label{eq:asimov-ts-reference-expectation}
\end{equation}
%
Equivalently, this is twice the generalized Kullback--Leibler
divergence between the two Poisson intensities,
%
\begin{equation}
    t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    2\int
    \left[
        \nu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
        -
        \nu_A
        +
        \nu_A\log
        \frac{\nu_A}{\nu_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}
    \right]d\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
%
which is non-negative and vanishes at
\(\boldsymbol{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\theta}_A\).

To evaluate Eq.~\eqref{eq:asimov-ts-reference-expectation}, we draw
\(M\) independent events
\(\mathbf{z}_m\sim q_{\phi}\).  These events form an empirical
quadrature of the reference density.  The corresponding weighted
Asimov data set has event weights
%
\begin{equation}
    w_m^A = \frac{H_A(\mathbf{z}_m)}{M},
\end{equation}
%
and the finite-sample estimator of the expected test statistic is
%
\begin{equation}
    \widehat{t}_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    2\left[
        \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\Lambda_A
    \right]
    +
    \frac{2}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_m),
    \label{eq:finite-asimov-ts}
\end{equation}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})
    \equiv
    H_A(\mathbf{z})
    \log
    \frac{H_A(\mathbf{z})}
         {H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z})}.
    \label{eq:Y-definition}
\end{equation}

For yield-only parameters, the minimum can be made exact even at finite
\(M\).  In particular, define ratios normalized on the same reference
sample,
%
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde r_j(\mathbf{z})
    =
    \frac{\widehat r_j(\mathbf{z})}
    {M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\widehat r_j(\mathbf{z}_m)},
    \qquad
    \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \widetilde r_j(\mathbf{z}_m)=1.
    \label{eq:empirical-ratio-normalization}
\end{equation}
%
If the parameters affect only the component yields, differentiation of
the finite Asimov log likelihood at the generating point gives
%
\begin{align}
    \left.
    \frac{\partial\widehat{\ell}_A}
         {\partial\theta_a}
    \right|_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_A}
    &=
    -\sum_j
    \frac{\partial\lambda_j}{\partial\theta_a}
    +
    \sum_j
    \frac{\partial\lambda_j}{\partial\theta_a}
    \frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \widetilde r_j(\mathbf{z}_m)
    \nonumber\\
    &=0.
    \label{eq:finite-score-cancellation}
\end{align}
%
Thus, the finite weighted construction fits the generating parameters
exactly for components whose shapes are fixed and whose yields are
varied.  Shape-dependent parameters generally retain a finite
quadrature error unless their parameter-dependent ratios are normalized
and integrated consistently.

The numerical precision of the test-statistic scan can be estimated
directly.  Conditional on fixed trained models and independent
reference events, the yield term in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:finite-asimov-ts} is deterministic, while the second term
is a sample mean.  Therefore,
%
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{Var}
    \left[
        \widehat t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    \right]
    =
    \frac{4}{M}
    \operatorname{Var}_{q_{\phi}}
    \left[
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \right],
\end{equation}
%
and
%
\begin{equation}
    \operatorname{SE}
    \left[
        \widehat t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    \right]
    \simeq
    \frac{2s_Y(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\sqrt{M}},
    \qquad
    s_Y^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    \frac{1}{M-1}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \left[
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_m)
        -
        \overline{Y}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \right]^2.
    \label{eq:asimov-mc-error}
\end{equation}
%
A pilot sample can consequently be used to estimate the required size:
to obtain a pointwise test-statistic uncertainty below
\(\epsilon_t\), one requires approximately
%
\begin{equation}
    M
    \gtrsim
    \max_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\ {\rm in\ scan}}
    \left[
        \frac{2s_Y(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\epsilon_t}
    \right]^2.
    \label{eq:required-asimov-size}
\end{equation}
%
For the low-dimensional, well-covered reference model considered here,
samples of order \(10^5\) events are generally sufficient to begin
quantitative convergence studies, while \(10^5\)--\(10^6\) events
provide a conservative range for a smooth scan.  The appropriate size
should nevertheless be established by repeating the construction at
increasing \(M\) and monitoring the fitted minimum, the scan curvature,
and the confidence-interval crossings.  When the same events are also
used to normalize the ratios, or when nuisance parameters are profiled,
a bootstrap of the complete normalization and profiling procedure
provides a more reliable uncertainty estimate than the pointwise
formula alone.

\subsection{Is a normalizing flow required for the Asimov construction?}
\label{sec:asimov-role-of-flow}

A tractable reference density is not mathematically required to
construct the weighted unbinned Asimov data set described above.  Let
\(\{\mathbf{z}_m\}_{m=1}^{M}\) instead be an ordinary MC sample drawn
from an implicit reference distribution \(q_{\rm ref}\).  Although
\(q_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{x})\) cannot be evaluated, classifiers can still
estimate the density ratios
%
\begin{equation}
    r_j(\mathbf{x})
    =
    \frac{p_j(\mathbf{x})}{q_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{x})}.
\end{equation}
%
Defining
%
\begin{equation}
    H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    =
    \sum_j
    \lambda_j(\boldsymbol{\theta})r_j(\mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}
%
the expected test-statistic scan can be estimated directly from the MC
reference sample:
%
\begin{equation}
    \widehat t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    2\left[
        \Lambda(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\Lambda_A
    \right]
    +
    \frac{2}{M}
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    H_A(\mathbf{z}_m)
    \log
    \frac{H_A(\mathbf{z}_m)}
         {H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}_m)}.
    \label{eq:mc-reference-asimov}
\end{equation}
%
The reference density itself does not need to be evaluated because it
cancels from the likelihood ratio:
%
\begin{equation}
    \log
    \frac{\nu_A(\mathbf{x})}
         {\nu(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta})}
    =
    \log
    \frac{
        q_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{x})H_A(\mathbf{x})
    }{
        q_{\rm ref}(\mathbf{x})
        H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    }
    =
    \log
    \frac{H_A(\mathbf{x})}
         {H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}.
    \label{eq:reference-cancellation}
\end{equation}
%
Furthermore, normalizing the density-ratio estimators on the same MC
sample ensures that the finite-sample score vanishes exactly at the
generating point for yield-only parameters, just as in the flow-based
construction.  Consequently, neither the existence of the weighted
Asimov data set nor its exact finite-sample minimum requires a
normalizing flow.

The advantage of the flow is therefore computational and operational
rather than conceptual.  Once trained, the flow can generate
arbitrarily large and statistically independent reference samples at a
cost much smaller than that of producing additional detector-level
simulation.  Separate samples can be generated for density-ratio
training, validation, Asimov integration, and pseudo-experiments,
avoiding correlations caused by repeatedly using the same finite MC
sample.  The flow also defines a continuous reference model, rather
than a discrete empirical distribution obtained by resampling stored
events.  Since the flow is a differentiable transformation of a simple
latent distribution, it may additionally permit variance-reduction
techniques such as antithetic sampling or low-discrepancy quadrature in
latent space.

These advantages should not, however, be interpreted as an increase in
the information provided by the original simulation.  Increasing the
number of events sampled from a fixed flow reduces the numerical
quadrature uncertainty, but it does not reduce the modeling uncertainty
of the learned flow and density ratios.  Schematically, the discrepancy
with respect to the simulator-level result contains two contributions,
%
\begin{equation}
    \widehat t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    -
    t_A^{\rm sim}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    =
    \delta t_{\rm model}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    +
    \delta t_{\rm quad}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
    \qquad
    \delta t_{\rm quad}
    =
    \mathcal{O}\!\left(M^{-1/2}\right).
    \label{eq:asimov-error-decomposition}
\end{equation}
%
The quadrature term vanishes as the number of flow samples increases,
whereas the modeling term does not.  A very large flow sample can
therefore determine the Asimov scan of the learned hybrid model with
high numerical precision without guaranteeing equal precision with
respect to the underlying simulator.

A sufficiently large MC reference sample is thus a valid, and
potentially less model-dependent, choice for the Asimov calculation.
Its limitation is that reducing its quadrature uncertainty requires
additional simulated events, which can be prohibitively expensive.
The contribution of the flow is to replace this repeatedly incurred
simulation cost by a reusable generative reference model.  More
broadly, the principal benefit of Hybrid NSBI is not the logical
possibility of constructing an Asimov data set, which is already
available through MC quadrature, but the simultaneous availability of
a continuous tractable density, inexpensive independent samples, and
accurate density-ratio corrections.  These properties make the same
model useful for likelihood evaluation, Asimov calculations, and
frequentist constructions based on pseudo-experiments.

\subsection{Variance-reduced flow quadratures}
\label{sec:variance-reduced-asimov}

The availability of a normalizing-flow reference model provides more
control over the numerical construction of an Asimov data set than
simply generating an arbitrarily large independent sample.  In
particular, the flow defines an explicit transport map
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{x}=T_\phi(\mathbf{z}),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{z}\sim
    p_0(\mathbf{z})
    =
    \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}),
    \label{eq:flow-transport-asimov}
\end{equation}
%
such that \(T_{\phi\#}p_0=q_\phi\).  The expectation entering the
Asimov test statistic can consequently be written in latent space as
%
\begin{align}
    I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    &\equiv
    \mathbb{E}_{q_\phi}
    \left[
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    \right]
    \nonumber\\
    &=
    \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}\sim p_0}
    \left[
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
        \bigl(T_\phi(\mathbf{z})\bigr)
    \right],
    \label{eq:asimov-latent-integral}
\end{align}
%
where
%
\begin{equation}
    Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    =
    H_A(\mathbf{x})
    \log
    \frac{H_A(\mathbf{x})}
         {H_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})}.
\end{equation}
%
For independent random samples, the variance of the estimator of
\(I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\) is
\(\sigma_Y^2(\boldsymbol{\theta})/M\).  The variance
\(\sigma_Y^2=\operatorname{Var}_{q_\phi}[Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}]\)
is a property of the integrand and reference distribution and cannot
be changed while retaining independent sampling from \(q_\phi\).
Nevertheless, the integration error can be reduced by replacing
independent sampling with a more efficient quadrature rule, or by
sampling from a different distribution and applying importance
weights.

A general quadrature approximation can be written as
%
\begin{equation}
    I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \simeq
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \omega_m
    Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_m),
    \qquad
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}\omega_m=1.
    \label{eq:general-flow-quadrature}
\end{equation}
%
The corresponding weighted Asimov events have weights
%
\begin{equation}
    w_m^A
    =
    \omega_m H_A(\mathbf{x}_m).
    \label{eq:general-asimov-weights}
\end{equation}
%
Ordinary Monte Carlo sampling is recovered by choosing independent
\(\mathbf{x}_m\sim q_\phi\) and \(\omega_m=1/M\).

\paragraph{Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo.}

A particularly natural possibility is to apply randomized
quasi-Monte Carlo sampling in the latent space of the flow
\cite{owen1997scrambled,andral2024combining}.  Writing
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}
    =
    \Phi^{-1}(\mathbf{u}),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^d,
\end{equation}
%
where \(\Phi^{-1}\) is the component-wise Gaussian inverse cumulative
distribution function, the quadrature points are constructed as
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{u}_m^{\mathrm{QMC}}
    \longrightarrow
    \mathbf{z}_m
    =
    \Phi^{-1}
    \bigl(\mathbf{u}_m^{\mathrm{QMC}}\bigr)
    \longrightarrow
    \mathbf{x}_m
    =
    T_\phi(\mathbf{z}_m).
    \label{eq:qmc-flow-transport}
\end{equation}
%
Low-discrepancy sequences, such as Sobol sequences, cover the latent
hypercube more uniformly than independent random points.  Transporting
these points through the flow provides a correspondingly uniform
quadrature of \(q_\phi\).  For sufficiently regular integrands, this
can produce convergence faster than the usual
\(\mathcal{O}(M^{-1/2})\) Monte Carlo rate
\cite{klebanov2023transporting}.

A deterministic quasi-Monte Carlo sequence does not directly provide
a statistical error estimate.  This can be addressed using randomized
scrambling.  Each scrambled sequence retains the low-discrepancy
structure, while independent scramblings provide replicate estimates.
If \(\widehat t_A^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\) is the test-statistic
scan obtained from scrambling \(r\), the combined result and its
estimated numerical uncertainty are
%
\begin{align}
    \overline t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    &=
    \frac{1}{R}
    \sum_{r=1}^{R}
    \widehat t_A^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),
    \\
    \widehat{\operatorname{SE}}
    \left[
        \overline t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
    \right]
    &=
    \left\{
        \frac{1}{R(R-1)}
        \sum_{r=1}^{R}
        \left[
            \widehat t_A^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
            -
            \overline t_A(\boldsymbol{\theta})
        \right]^2
    \right\}^{1/2}.
    \label{eq:rqmc-scan-error}
\end{align}
%
The same points should be retained throughout a parameter scan.  The
resulting correlations between scan points suppress artificial
point-to-point fluctuations and provide a smooth estimate of the
likelihood curve.  Since quasi-Monte Carlo points are deliberately
dependent, the ordinary estimate \(2s_Y/\sqrt{M}\) does not apply; the
dispersion among independent scramblings should be used instead.

\paragraph{Gaussian quadrature and sparse grids.}

The Gaussian base distribution also permits deterministic
Gauss--Hermite quadrature:
%
\begin{equation}
    I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \simeq
    \sum_{m=1}^{M}
    \omega_m^{\mathrm{GH}}
    Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    \bigl(T_\phi(\mathbf{z}_m^{\mathrm{GH}})\bigr).
    \label{eq:gauss-hermite-flow}
\end{equation}
%
In five dimensions, a tensor-product rule with \(n\) nodes per
dimension requires \(n^5\) evaluations; for example,
\(8^5=32768\) and \(10^5=100000\).  Sparse-grid constructions can
reduce this number further.  Transport maps have previously been
proposed as a means of applying higher-order quadrature rules and
sparse grids to complex target distributions
\cite{klebanov2023transporting}.

Such a construction would produce a deterministic Asimov quadrature,
with convergence assessed by increasing the quadrature order rather
than through a sampling variance.  Its performance depends on the
regularity of the composed latent-space integrand
\(Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\circ T_\phi\).  Sharp features introduced by
the density-ratio estimators or by tail regions may reduce the
advantage of high-order quadrature.  Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo is
therefore expected to be more robust, while Gaussian and sparse-grid
quadratures may be particularly effective in low-dimensional and
smooth examples.

\paragraph{Antithetic latent sampling.}

The symmetry of the Gaussian base distribution provides a simpler
variance-reduction strategy.  Each latent point can be paired with its
negative,
%
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{z}
    \longleftrightarrow
    -\mathbf{z},
    \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_{+}=T_\phi(\mathbf{z}),
    \qquad
    \mathbf{x}_{-}=T_\phi(-\mathbf{z}).
\end{equation}
%
The integral is then estimated using pair averages,
%
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{+})
        +
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}_{-})
    \right].
\end{equation}
%
For a fixed total number of evaluations, the variance relative to
independent sampling is multiplied by \(1+\rho\), where \(\rho\) is
the correlation between the two members of the pair.  Antithetic
sampling is beneficial when this correlation is negative, but the
nonlinear flow transformation does not guarantee this property.  Its
effectiveness can therefore be determined with a small pilot study.

\paragraph{Importance sampling targeted to the likelihood scan.}

A more aggressive strategy is to change the sampling distribution.
For an alternative proposal \(g(\mathbf{x})\),
%
\begin{equation}
    I_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
    =
    \mathbb{E}_{g}
    \left[
        \frac{q_\phi(\mathbf{x})}
             {g(\mathbf{x})}
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    \right].
    \label{eq:asimov-importance-sampling}
\end{equation}
%
For a single parameter point, the proposal that minimizes the
importance-sampling variance is formally
%
\begin{equation}
    g_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^\star(\mathbf{x})
    \propto
    q_\phi(\mathbf{x})
    \left|
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})
    \right|.
    \label{eq:optimal-asimov-proposal}
\end{equation}
%
For a collection of scan points \(\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_k\}\), a
single proposal minimizing a weighted sum of second moments is
%
\begin{equation}
    g^\star(\mathbf{x})
    \propto
    q_\phi(\mathbf{x})
    \left[
        \sum_k
        a_k
        Y_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_k}^2(\mathbf{x})
    \right]^{1/2},
    \label{eq:optimal-scan-proposal}
\end{equation}
%
where the coefficients \(a_k\) can emphasize the regions relevant for
a discovery test or confidence-interval crossing.  Since both
sampling and density evaluation are available for \(q_\phi\), an
auxiliary flow could be trained to approximate this optimized proposal
\cite{muller2019neuralimportance}.  In practice, a defensive mixture
%
\begin{equation}
    g_\epsilon
    =
    (1-\epsilon)g^\star+\epsilon q_\phi
\end{equation}
%
would protect against incomplete phase-space coverage and excessively
large importance weights.

Finally, the exact finite-quadrature normalization discussed above can
be retained for all these constructions.  Given quadrature weights
\(\omega_m\), define
%
\begin{equation}
    \widetilde r_j(\mathbf{x})
    =
    \frac{\widehat r_j(\mathbf{x})}
    {\sum_m\omega_m\widehat r_j(\mathbf{x}_m)},
    \qquad
    \sum_m
    \omega_m
    \widetilde r_j(\mathbf{x}_m)=1.
    \label{eq:weighted-quadrature-normalization}
\end{equation}
%
Using these normalized ratios together with the Asimov weights in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:general-asimov-weights} preserves the exact
finite-quadrature cancellation of the score for yield-only
parameters.  Randomized Sobol sampling in latent space appears to
offer the most direct and robust improvement for the present
low-dimensional example.  Higher-order quadrature and
likelihood-targeted importance sampling provide promising extensions
when still greater numerical precision is required.  These
variance-reduction possibilities are enabled by the transport-map
structure of the normalizing flow, but are not used in the numerical
results presented below.

\subsection{One signal-strength parameter}

For the demonstration, consider
\begin{equation}
  \widehat\nu( x\mid\mu)
  =\qRef( x)
   \left[\mu\lambda_S\widetilde r_S( x)
   +\lambda_B\widetilde r_B( x)\right].
  \label{eq:mu-model}
\end{equation}
At $\mu_A$, define
\begin{equation}
  Q_m=\frac{\lambda_S\widetilde r_S( z_m)}
             {\lambda_B\widetilde r_B( z_m)}.
\end{equation}
Dropping terms independent of $\mu$, the Asimov negative log likelihood is
\begin{equation}
  \widetilde{\mathrm{NLL}}_A(\mu)
  =2\left[
    \mu\lambda_S-
    \sum_m w_m^A\log(1+\mu Q_m)
  \right].
  \label{eq:relative-asimov-nll}
\end{equation}
Because
\begin{equation}
  w_m^A\frac{Q_m}{1+\mu_AQ_m}
  =\frac{\lambda_S\widetilde r_S( z_m)}{M},
\end{equation}
the score at the generating point is
\begin{equation}
  \left.\frac{\partial\widetilde{\mathrm{NLL}}_A}{\partial\mu}
  \right|_{\mu_A}
  =2\lambda_S\left[1-\frac{1}{M}
  \sum_m\widetilde r_S( z_m)\right]=0.
  \label{eq:asimov-score-zero}
\end{equation}
The second derivative is nonnegative.  Thus the empirical weighted Asimov dataset has its global minimum at $\mu_A$ in the physical region, up to numerical optimizer tolerance.

\subsection{Expected uncertainty and toy distributions}

Following Cowan \emph{et al.}~\cite{Cowan:2010js}, evaluate the discovery statistic $q_0$ on an Asimov dataset generated at $\mu_A>0$:
\begin{equation}
  q_{0,A}=-2\log\frac{\Lcal_A(0)}{\Lcal_A(\widehat\mu_A)}.
\end{equation}
In the one-parameter Wald approximation,
\begin{equation}
  q_{0,A}\simeq\frac{\mu_A^2}{\sigma_A^2},
  \qquad
  \sigma_A=\frac{\mu_A}{\sqrt{q_{0,A}}},
  \qquad
  Z_A=\sqrt{q_{0,A}}.
  \label{eq:asimov-sigma}
\end{equation}
The pseudo-experiment distribution of $\muhat$ can be compared with the Gaussian prediction of width $\sigma_A$.  For an unconstrained two-sided statistic, $t_0$ approaches a noncentral $\chi^2$ distribution with one degree of freedom and noncentrality $q_{0,A}$.  With the physical constraint $\muhat\geq0$, the discovery statistic is instead represented asymptotically by
\begin{equation}
  q_0=\left[\max(0,Y)\right]^2,
  \qquad Y\sim\Normal(\sqrt{q_{0,A}},1),
  \label{eq:boundary-q0}
\end{equation}
which includes a point mass $\Phi(-\sqrt{q_{0,A}})$ at zero.

#For multiple normalization parameters or nuisance parameters, $w_m^A$ is still given by \cref{eq:asimov-weights}.  The symbol differentiated in the score is simply a component of $\theta$.  The expected score vector vanishes at $\theta_A$, and the inverse expected Hessian gives the asymptotic covariance matrix after profiling.  The construction is therefore not restricted to one parameter; the one-dimensional derivation merely makes the cancellation transparent.

\section{Five-dimensional demonstration}
\label{sec:demonstration}

We implement the hybrid construction in Exercise~5 of the ML4HEP 2026 conference tutorial~\cite{HybridNSBI:2026code,Sandesara:2026lectures}.  The present section records the frozen analysis design.  Numerical values and final figures will be inserted after the long training run is complete; placeholders are deliberately retained so that the text does not imply results that have not yet been reviewed.

\subsection{Samples and preselection}

The toy measurement contains signal and background events described by five latent variables.  Each process is a correlated Gaussian mixture with two process-specific modes and a common broad component.  Independent Gaussian detector smearing produces the reconstructed inputs $ x=(x_1,\ldots,x_5)$.  The common broad component guarantees overlapping analytic support and makes exact reconstructed densities available for validation.  The generated samples contain $10^8$ background and $2\times10^7$ signal events, with inclusive expected yields
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_B^{\mathrm{incl}}=10^6,
  \qquad
  \lambda_S^{\mathrm{incl}}=1.1\times10^3.
\end{equation}

A classifier preselection is trained with at most $5\times10^5$ events per class using three hidden layers of 256 nodes.  The threshold on its estimate of $p_S/p_B$ is selected so that the expected post-selection ratio is approximately $B/S=250$.  The preselection is not part of the final likelihood; it defines the phase-space region in which all densities are normalized.  Deterministic row hashes produce disjoint preselection-training, model-training, and final-evaluation partitions, and the parquet samples are streamed so the full event sets need not reside in memory.

\subsection{Reference flow}

After preselection, $5\times10^5$ signal and $5\times10^5$ background events are concatenated with equal component normalization.  A normalizing flow is trained by unweighted maximum likelihood on this balanced sample.  The chosen model has ten quadratic-spline coupling transforms.  Each conditioner has four hidden layers with 1024 features; the splines use 16 bins over the standardized interval $[-5,5]$ and linear tails outside it.  Training uses batches of 2048, an initial learning rate of $10^{-4}$, and at most 70 epochs with early stopping.

Because a smooth flow can place small probability outside the hard preselection boundary, generated events are passed through the same preselection classifier and rejected if they fail.  Five million accepted events are retained as a fixed unweighted reference sample.  The corresponding accepted reference density includes the flow acceptance normalization.  Validation includes one- and two-dimensional closure, correlation closure, the distribution and cumulative distribution of the learned log density, and a direct comparison with the analytic balanced-mixture density.

\section{Discussion and outlook}
\label{sec:discussion}

Hybrid NSBI changes the role of absolute density estimation.  Training an independent flow for every process asks each model to be accurate enough that the ratio of two imperfect densities supports a precision measurement.  The hybrid approach asks one flow to cover a reference phase space and then learns every precision-critical correction discriminatively.  Because the denominator examples are drawn from the trained flow, classifier ratios can correct the flow's local density distortions.  At the same time, the explicit $\qRef$ factor turns the original ratio representation into a normalized density model.

This construction addresses the two operational limitations discussed in \Cref{sec:ratio-limitations}.  The flow can generate arbitrarily many independent reference points, so numerical quadrature for profiles, impacts, and Asimov calculations is not capped by the size of the original reference MC.  The normalized ratio weights turn the same proposal into process-specific samplers, enabling pseudo-experiments and Neyman constructions without a new generative model for every parameter point.  The original parametrized nuisance model can be retained, while more flexible nonparametric parameter dependence can be added at the ratio layer.

Several limitations remain and should be treated as part of the method definition.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Support and importance-weight variance.}  A flow that misses a relevant region creates extreme ratios and low effective sample size.  Balanced-mixture design, tail modeling, ratio-tail monitoring, and process-wise effective sample sizes are mandatory diagnostics.
  \item \emph{Classifier misspecification.}  Empirical normalization fixes only the integral.  Calibration, reweighting, ensemble stability, and inference-level closure against independent samples remain necessary.  Neyman construction is especially valuable when residual neural bias could invalidate simple asymptotic assumptions.
  \item \emph{Finite reference quadrature.}  The weighted Asimov dataset is exactly stationary for the empirical hybrid model, not automatically for the unknown data-generating process.  Reference-sample enlargement and repeated-flow or repeated-ratio studies can separate quadrature, flow, and classifier uncertainties.
  \item \emph{Negative-weight and interference components.}  Individual signed contributions are not probability densities and cannot be sampled directly.  A practical implementation must use positive physical combinations as sampling channels, while retaining analytic signed coefficients in the intensity, as is already done in parametrized NSBI.
  \item \emph{Nuisance-parameter coverage.}  A nominal reference must cover the support of all relevant systematic variations.  Very large shape changes may require a broader mixture or a conditional reference flow.
\end{itemize}

The reference distribution can itself be optimized.  Equal process normalization is a robust first choice, but the objective could incorporate worst-case effective sample size over components and parameter points.  Mixtures of flows, heavier-tailed bases, or conditional proposals may be preferable in high dimensions.  These choices affect computational efficiency, not the algebraic validity of \cref{eq:hybrid-density}, provided support is maintained and ratios are trained against the proposal actually used.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have introduced hybrid neural simulation-based inference, a construction that combines a tractable normalizing-flow reference with high-precision classifier estimates of process-to-reference density ratios.  Sampling the denominator class from the learned flow is essential: it makes the ratio the correction to that specific tractable proposal, so local flow error cancels in the ideal classifier limit.  The resulting process densities can be evaluated, integrated, and sampled while inference retains the ratio structure of parametrized NSBI.

The hybrid representation supplies two statistical objects that are cumbersome in a ratio-only analysis: a deterministic weighted unbinned Asimov measure and an efficient process sampler for pseudo-experiments.  The extended part of the Asimov likelihood is enforced by matching the total event weight to the expected yield, and ratio normalization on the same reference quadrature guarantees the fitted generating value for the one-parameter demonstration.  The Asimov value of $q_0$ then predicts the estimator width and the noncentral toy distribution in the usual Wald regime.

The five-dimensional conference exercise is designed as an inference-level proof of concept, with analytic truth available for closure.  Once its long training run is complete, the final manuscript will report the reference and hybrid-density diagnostics, the comparison of hybrid and analytic likelihood scans, the exact empirical Asimov closure, and the agreement between Asimov predictions and pseudo-experiment distributions.  These tests will determine whether the proposed division of labor---generative coverage from a flow and precision corrections from ratios---delivers the expected practical advantage.
